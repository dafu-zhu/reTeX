The general proof of (6.43) follows similarly.

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & a & b \\ a & 1 & c \\ b & c & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ be a
$3 \times 3$ symmetric positive definite matrix. From (5.63), we obtain that
\begin{equation}
-1 < a < 1 \quad \text{and} \quad 1 + 2abc > a^2 + b^2 + c^2.
\end{equation}
If $U = \begin{pmatrix} U(1,1) & U(1,2) & U(1,3) \\ 0 & U(2,2) & U(2,3) \\ 0 & 0 &
U(3,3) \end{pmatrix}$ is the Cholesky factor of $A$, then, from the first step of the
Cholesky decomposition algorithm, see (6.6) and (6.8), we find that
\[
U(1,1) \;=\; \sqrt{A(1,1)} \;=\; 1; \quad U(1,2) \;=\; \frac{A(1,2)}{U(1,1)} \;=\; a;
\quad U(1,3) \;=\; \frac{A(1,3)}{U(1,1)} \;=\; b.
\]
Thus, the current form of $U$ is
\begin{equation}
U \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 1 & a & b \\ 0 & U(2,2) & U(2,3) \\ 0 & 0 & U(3,3) \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Then, from (6.15), we find that the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} U(2,2) & U(2,3) \\ 0 &
U(3,3) \end{pmatrix}$ is the Cholesky factor of the following matrix:
\begin{align*}
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & c \\ c & 1 \end{pmatrix} \;-\; \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} a & b \end{pmatrix}
&= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & c \\ c & 1 \end{pmatrix} \;-\; \begin{pmatrix} a^2 & ab \\ ab & b^2 \end{pmatrix} \\
&= \begin{pmatrix} 1-a^2 & c-ab \\ c-ab & 1-b^2 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{align*}
Note that the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1-a^2 & c-ab \\ c-ab & 1-b^2 \end{pmatrix}$ from
(6.47) satisfies the conditions (5.49) and is therefore symmetric positive definite:
from (6.45), it follows that $1-a^2 > 0$, since $-1 < a < 1$, and that
\[
(1-a^2)(1-b^2) - (c-ab)^2 \;=\; 1+2abc-a^2-b^2-c^2 \;>\; 0.
\]
Since $\begin{pmatrix} U(2,2) & U(2,3) \\ 0 & U(3,3) \end{pmatrix}$ is the Cholesky
factor of $\begin{pmatrix} 1-a^2 & c-ab \\ c-ab & 1-b^2 \end{pmatrix}$, we obtain from
(6.41) that
\begin{align*}
\begin{pmatrix} U(2,2) & U(2,3) \\ 0 & U(3,3) \end{pmatrix}
&= \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{1-a^2} & \dfrac{c-ab}{\sqrt{1-a^2}} \\[0.6em]
0 & \sqrt{\dfrac{(1-a^2)(1-b^2)-(c-ab)^2}{1-a^2}} \end{pmatrix} \\
&= \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{1-a^2} & (c-ab)/\sqrt{1-a^2} \\[0.6em]
0 & \sqrt{\dfrac{1+2abc-a^2-b^2-c^2}{1-a^2}} \end{pmatrix}.
\end{align*}
From (6.46) and (6.48), we conclude that the Cholesky factor of the matrix $A$ is
\begin{equation}
U \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 1 & a & b \\ 0 & \sqrt{1-a^2} & (c-ab)/\sqrt{1-a^2} \\[0.6em]
0 & 0 & \sqrt{\dfrac{1+2abc-a^2-b^2-c^2}{1-a^2}} \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
which is the same as (6.44).
\end{proof}


\section{Linear solvers for symmetric positive definite matrices}

Using the Cholesky decomposition of a matrix is a computationally efficient way of
solving linear systems corresponding to symmetric positive definite matrices.

Let $A$ be a symmetric positive definite matrix of size $n$, and let $b$ be a column
vector of size $n$. If $A = U\tp U$ is the Cholesky decomposition of $A$, then, solving
a linear system $Ax = b$ is equivalent to solving
\[
U\tp Ux \;=\; b.
\]
This is the same as solving
\[
U\tp y \;=\; b
\]
for $y$, which can be done using forward substitution since the matrix $U\tp$ is lower
triangular, and then solving
\[
Ux = y
\]
for $x$, which can be done using backward substitution since the matrix $U$ is upper
triangular.

The pseudocode for solving a linear system corresponding to a symmetric positive
definite matrix using the Cholesky decomposition can be found in Table 6.2.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Linear solver using Cholesky decomposition}
\fbox{\begin{minipage}{0.85\linewidth}
\textbf{Function Call:}\\
$x = \text{linear\_solve\_cholesky}(A,b)$
\bigskip

\textbf{Input:}\\
$A = $ symmetric positive definite matrix of size $n$\\
$b = $ column vector of size $n$
\bigskip

\textbf{Output:}\\
$x = $ solution to $Ax = b$
\bigskip

\noindent
$U = \text{cholesky}(A)$; \hfill\textit{// Cholesky decomposition of $A$}\\
$y = \text{forward\_subst}(U\tp,b)$; \hfill\textit{// solve $U\tp y = b$}\\
$x = \text{backward\_subst}(U,y)$; \hfill\textit{// solve $Ux = y$}
\end{minipage}}
\end{table}
